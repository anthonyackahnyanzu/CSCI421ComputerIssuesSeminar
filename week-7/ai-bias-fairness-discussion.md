# Week 7 Class Materials
## Topic: Artificial Intelligence, Bias, and Fairness

**Syllabus alignment (Week 7):**
- Core theme: **AI ethics in practice**
- Guiding question: **How can we use AI to improve society while preventing unfairness, abuse, and loss of human agency?**

---

## 1) Short Opening Presentation (8–12 minutes)

### A. Framing the Week
- AI systems are now part of hiring, lending, health triage, education, policing, warfare, and everyday information access.
- Ethical analysis must go beyond “does it work?” and include:
  - **Who benefits?**
  - **Who bears risk?**
  - **Who is accountable when harm occurs?**

### B. Key Concepts for Today
1. **Bias in AI**
   - Data bias (historical inequities baked into data)
   - Measurement bias (poor proxies for complex human realities)
   - Deployment bias (using a model in contexts it was not designed for)

2. **Fairness**
   - Equal treatment may still produce unequal outcomes.
   - Fairness requires context-specific choices and trade-offs.

3. **Human agency**
   - AI can support decisions, but people should keep meaningful control over life-impacting outcomes.

### C. Economic Advantages of Responsible AI
- **Productivity growth:** automation of repetitive tasks can free time for creative and high-value work.
- **Lower costs:** better forecasting, logistics, and process optimization can reduce waste.
- **Improved access:** AI tools can scale tutoring, translation, and early diagnostics to underserved communities.
- **Innovation spillovers:** new industries, startups, and jobs in model governance, auditing, safety, and human-centered design.

### D. Novel Ways AI Can Improve Society
- **Public-interest AI for local governments:** optimize energy use, reduce water loss, and improve emergency response routing.
- **Bias-checking copilots in institutions:** flag potentially discriminatory language or decisions in hiring, housing, and lending workflows before final action.
- **AI for accessibility:** real-time captioning, visual description, and adaptive interfaces for people with disabilities.
- **Civic deliberation support:** summarize public comments transparently so large communities can participate more effectively in policy processes.

### E. Ethical Warning
AI can also intensify inequality, surveillance, and coercion if deployed without safeguards. Ethical AI is not only technical—it is political, economic, and institutional.

## 2) Group Discussion Activity (3 Groups)

Break into **three groups**. Each group will:
1. Discuss assigned questions.
2. Produce a short deliverable.
3. Recommend concrete policies.

### Group 1 — AI Relationships, Emotional Dependency, and Ethical Risk
**Focus:** Human-AI relationships (companions, chatbots, parasocial bonds), emotional manipulation, and autonomy.

**Discussion questions:**
1. When does an AI companion become psychologically beneficial, and when does it become harmful dependency?
2. Is it ethical for AI systems to simulate affection, intimacy, or friendship without clear disclosure?
3. Should AI relationship systems have guardrails for minors or vulnerable users?
4. What obligations do companies have when users show signs of crisis, obsession, or isolation?
5. Where should we draw boundaries between therapeutic support and exploitative engagement design?

**Deliverable:**
- Group spokesperson report-out: a verbal **“AI Relationship Ethics Charter”** including:
  - 3 red lines (unacceptable practices),
  - 3 positive design principles,
  - 2 user protections for vulnerable populations.

**Policy recommendations to include:**
- Mandatory disclosure that users are interacting with AI.
- Prohibition on monetizing emotional dependency patterns.
- Escalation protocols for self-harm or severe crisis signals.

---

### Group 2 — AI Weapons, Military Use, and the Anthropic–Pentagon Debate
**Focus:** Autonomous weapons, military contracting, dual-use AI, and limits on state power.

#### Quick Primer: Anthropic and the Pentagon Tension
Public debate has highlighted tensions between AI labs and defense institutions over how frontier models should be used in national security contexts. A key concern is whether collaboration can remain bounded to defensive, analytical, and non-lethal support uses—or drift toward high-risk military autonomy.

**Two emphasized principles associated with Anthropic’s stance in public discussions:**
1. **No autonomous weapons** (humans must remain responsible for lethal decisions).
2. **No mass surveillance** (AI should not enable population-scale rights violations).

**Discussion questions:**
1. Should any AI company support defense projects if strict constraints are in place?
2. Is a “human-in-the-loop” requirement enough, or can it become symbolic rather than meaningful?
3. What is the ethical difference between defensive military applications and offensive autonomous targeting?
4. How can society verify that “no autonomous weapons” commitments are actually enforced?
5. How should democratic oversight work for military AI procurement and deployment?

**Deliverable:**
- Group spokesperson report-out with two parts:
  1. “Permitted vs Prohibited Military AI Uses” table,
  2. Accountability framework (audits, reporting, and enforcement triggers).

**Policy recommendations to include:**
- Binding prohibition on fully autonomous lethal targeting.
- Explicit ban on AI-enabled mass civilian surveillance.
- Independent audits with public summaries for high-risk defense AI contracts.

---

### Group 3 — User Agency vs AI Nudging for Lawful Behavior
**Focus:** Should AI systems steer users away from illegal actions, and how far should that steering go?

**Discussion questions:**
1. What is the line between legitimate safety intervention and paternalistic control?
2. Should AI refuse all potentially illegal requests, or provide legal alternatives and education?
3. Who decides what counts as “harmful” or “illegal” across jurisdictions?
4. Could over-restrictive nudging reduce user agency, creativity, or legitimate dissent?
5. What transparency should users get about why outputs were blocked, redirected, or constrained?

**Deliverable:**
- Group spokesperson report-out of a **“User Agency and Safety Governance Matrix”** with:
  - 3 levels of intervention (warn, refuse, escalate),
  - examples for each level,
  - required transparency language to users.

**Policy recommendations to include:**
- Explainable refusals (clear reason + safe alternative path).
- Appeals process for users and researchers.
- Public policy documentation for high-impact safety constraints.

## 3) Whole-Class Synthesis Prompts
- Which group identified the hardest trade-off between innovation and rights?
- Where should we require hard legal bans vs flexible ethical guidelines?
- What harms are most urgent: bias, coercion, autonomous violence, or surveillance?
- What is one policy your group would implement immediately on campus, in industry, or in government?

## 4) Additional High-Interest AI Ethics Topics (Optional if time allows)
- **AI and labor transitions:** who is displaced, who gains, and what retraining obligations exist?
- **Environmental ethics of AI:** energy use, compute concentration, and sustainability standards.
- **Global fairness:** who gets to define AI norms—high-income countries, corporations, or multilateral institutions?
- **Synthetic media and trust:** watermarking, provenance, and democratic resilience against deepfakes.
