# Week 15 Class Materials
## Topic: AI Automation, Jobs, and the Future of Work

**Syllabus alignment (Week 15):**
- Core theme: **Technological disruption, labor transitions, and social responsibility**
- Guiding question: **How can society capture the benefits of AI automation while protecting people from unfair job displacement and economic insecurity?**

---

### A. Framing the Week
- AI automation is changing work in administration, customer support, logistics, software, media, education, and healthcare.
- The ethical question is not only whether jobs disappear, but:
  - **Which tasks are automated first?**
  - **Who benefits from productivity gains?**
  - **Who carries the transition costs?**
- Technology can raise living standards, but only if institutions (schools, employers, government) adapt fast enough.

### B. Key Concepts for Today
1. **Task automation vs job replacement**
   - Most innovation automates parts of jobs before replacing whole occupations.
   - New roles can emerge, but timing matters: workers can be displaced before new pathways are available.

2. **Creative destruction**
   - Innovation can eliminate older forms of work while creating new sectors.
   - Without policy support, gains may concentrate while losses are socialized.

3. **Transition justice**
   - Fair transitions require retraining access, wage support, geographic mobility options, and worker voice.

### C. Historical Perspective: Past Innovations and Job Impacts
- **Industrial mechanization:** reduced some manual labor but expanded factory and supply-chain work.
- **Electrification and mass production:** transformed skilled trades and created large middle-skill labor markets.
- **Computers and office software:** automated clerical tasks while expanding IT, analytics, and digital services.
- **Internet platforms:** disrupted retail/media jobs while creating e-commerce, platform operations, and new creator economies.

### D. Ethical Warning
- If AI productivity gains are captured only by firms and capital owners, inequality can deepen even as total output rises.
- “The market will sort it out” may not protect workers in high-speed disruptions.

## 2) Group Discussion Activity (3 Groups)

Break into **three groups**. Each group will:
1. Discuss assigned questions.
2. Create one practical response plan.
3. Prepare to defend trade-offs during whole-class debate.

### Group 1 — Lessons from Past Innovations
**Focus:** What history teaches us about job disruption and adaptation.

**Discussion questions:**
1. Which past innovation (mechanization, electricity, computers, internet) caused the most disruptive labor transition, and why?
2. In those transitions, which workers were protected and which were left behind?
3. What policies or institutions helped people adapt (unions, public education, apprenticeships, unemployment insurance)?
4. Are AI-driven changes likely to be faster than previous waves? How does speed change ethical responsibility?
5. If we could repeat one historical transition differently, what would we redesign?

**Active discussion prompts:**
- “I agree with ___ because…” then add one historical example.
- “A missing perspective is…” and explain whose experience is not represented.

**Deliverable:**
- A **“History-to-Policy Lessons Map”** with:
  - 3 lessons from past innovations,
  - 2 mistakes society should not repeat,
  - 2 policy ideas for today.

---

### Group 2 — Who Gains, Who Loses in AI Automation?
**Focus:** Distribution of risk and reward across workers, firms, and communities.

**Discussion questions:**
1. Which sectors on campus/local economy seem most vulnerable to near-term AI automation?
2. Which types of workers face the highest risk (entry-level, routine cognitive, gig workers, etc.)?
3. Who benefits most from productivity gains right now, and is that distribution fair?
4. Should firms deploying labor-saving AI have obligations to retrain current workers before layoffs?
5. What protections should exist for workers whose jobs are partially automated but not eliminated?

**Active discussion prompts:**
- “Let’s test that assumption—what evidence would support or challenge it?”
- “If your policy was implemented tomorrow, who benefits first and who might be harmed?”

**Deliverable:**
- A **“Risk and Reward Equity Brief”** including:
  - 3 high-risk groups,
  - 3 potential beneficiaries,
  - a fairness scorecard for one AI deployment scenario.

---

### Group 3 — Solutions if AI Automates Too Many Jobs
**Focus:** Practical solutions students think could reduce harm and strengthen social stability.

**Discussion questions:**
1. If AI automates too many jobs too quickly, what should come first: retraining, shorter workweek, wage insurance, public jobs, or UBI-style support?
2. What is one realistic solution for the next 2 years, and one structural solution for the next 10 years?
3. Should taxation of AI-driven productivity gains help fund transition programs? Why or why not?
4. What role should universities play in preparing students for continual job transitions?
5. How should workers, not just companies, participate in deciding where automation is deployed?

**Active discussion prompts:**
- “Build on that idea: what would implementation look like in practice?”
- “What unintended consequence could this solution create, and how would we mitigate it?”

**Deliverable:**
- A **“Student Transition Strategy”** with:
  - 3 short-term safeguards,
  - 3 long-term reforms,
  - 2 funding approaches,
  - 1 accountability mechanism.

---

## 3) Whole-Class Synthesis Prompts
- Which proposal best balances innovation with worker dignity?
- What should be the minimum social guarantee during AI-driven displacement?
- Which actor has the greatest responsibility: government, employers, universities, or workers themselves?
- Should society slow certain forms of automation until transition systems are in place?

## 4) Rapid Policy Design Challenge (Optional if time allows)
Draft a one-page memo titled:
**“Fair AI Automation Compact.”**

Include:
1. One principle that protects innovation.
2. One principle that protects workers.
3. One measurable transition goal for 24 months.
4. One funding mechanism.
5. One enforcement mechanism.

## 5) Exit Ticket
In 5–7 sentences, respond to all three:
1. What is one historical innovation that gives you a useful comparison for AI and jobs?
2. What is one group you think is most vulnerable to AI labor disruption, and why?
3. What is one solution you believe is both ethical and politically realistic?
